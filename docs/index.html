Pricing the Future

How much is a tonne of CO₂ worth avoiding today? An empirical answer that estimates climate damages, calibrates four discounting methods to data, computes the social cost of carbon, and tests whether markets price temperature risk.

1How temperature affects economic growth

A panel of 185 countries, 1961–2025, with country and year fixed effects: each country's year-to-year weather shocks identify the effect. Hover over a dot to see a country.

Warming raises growth in cold countries and lowers it in hot ones; the growth-maximising temperature is about 8°C. Poor countries' damage curve is 3.6× as steep as the average, with an optimum near 16°C.

Full analysis: 01_damages.ipynb

2Four ways to discount the far future

Forward discount rates implied by constant, gamma (Weitzman), stochastic (Newell–Pizer) and consumption-based (Ramsey SDF) discounting, each calibrated to data. Click legend entries to hide or show methods.

US interest rates are highly persistent (φ = 0.92); a random walk cannot be rejected (t = -1.7 vs. a −2.9 critical value). Under the random walk, long-run rates fall towards zero and $1 of damage in 300 years is worth 85× more today than under mean reversion.

Full analysis: 02_discounting.ipynb

3The social cost of carbon

Damages from one extra tonne emitted today, discounted under each method, with 95% confidence intervals (cut off where they run past the axis). Use the menu to switch the damage specification and whether lost growth is ever recovered.

The method moves the SCC from $82 (Ramsey SDF, Nordhaus, climate beta = 1) to $14,618 (Newell-Pizer random walk, US); the official ≈$174 sits inside that range. Whether lost growth is recovered matters as much: $13 (level effect) vs $1,136 (growth effect) at a constant rate. Every 95% confidence interval includes zero: the damage function is too imprecise to rule out a zero SCC.

Full analysis: 03_scc.ipynb

4Is temperature risk priced in stock returns?

Response of each of the 49 Fama–French industries to a 1°C temperature surprise, controlling for market, size and value. Blue = significant at 5%.

Only 4 of 49 industries respond significantly (about 2 expected by chance), and Fama–MacBeth finds no significant price of temperature risk (t = 0.56). Markets, like the consumption model, attach little premium to short-run weather risk.
